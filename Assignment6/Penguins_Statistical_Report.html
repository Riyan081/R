1. Introduction

The Palmer Penguins dataset (Horst, Hill & Gorman; 344 penguins, 3 species: Adelie, Chinstrap, Gentoo) was analyzed to determine whether body mass and flipper length differ significantly across species and sex. After removing rows with missing values in the variables used (species, sex, body_mass_g, flipper_length_mm), 333 of 344 penguins remained for analysis.

3. Hypothesis Testing — Body Mass by Sex

H0: μ(male) = μ(female) H1: μ(male) ≠ μ(female)

  • Shapiro-Wilk: male W = 0.925, p < .001; female W = 0.919, p < .001 — both reject normality, but with n = 168 (male) and n = 165 (female), the t-test remains valid by the Central Limit Theorem.
  • Levene’s test: F(1,331) = 6.06, p = .014 — variances are not equal, so Welch’s t-test is the appropriate choice.
  • Welch two-sample t-test: t(323.9) = 8.55, p < .001. Mean male mass = 4545.7 g, mean female mass = 3862.3 g, difference = 683.4 g.
  • 95% CI for the difference: [526.2, 840.6] g — does not contain 0.
  • Cohen’s d = 0.94 (large effect).

Interpretation: The extremely small p-value and a CI that excludes zero confirm a real difference in mean body mass between sexes, and Cohen’s d = 0.94 shows this is a large, practically meaningful difference, not just a statistically detectable one.

4. One-Way ANOVA — Body Mass by Species

  • Shapiro-Wilk per species: Adelie p = .042, Chinstrap p = .56, Gentoo p = .26 — Adelie shows a mild deviation from normality but with n = 146 the ANOVA is robust to this.
  • Levene’s test: F(2,330) = 5.13, p = .006 — variances differ somewhat across species, motivating the Kruskal-Wallis cross-check in Section 5.
  • ANOVA: F(2, 330) = 341.9, p < .001 (species has a highly significant effect on body mass).

Interpretation: Gentoo is significantly heavier than both other species, while Adelie and Chinstrap are statistically indistinguishable in body mass.

5. Non-Parametric Analysis — Kruskal-Wallis

Kruskal-Wallis: χ² = 212.1, df = 2, p < .001.

Comparison with ANOVA: Both tests reach the same conclusion — species significantly affects body mass. The agreement between the parametric and non-parametric approaches shows the ANOVA result is robust despite the mild variance heterogeneity flagged by Levene’s test.

This makes flipper length a more discriminating physical trait for species identification, even though the overall species ordering (Gentoo > Chinstrap > Adelie) matches body mass.

8. Overall Conclusions

  1. Body mass differs significantly by species (Gentoo >> Adelie ≈ Chinstrap) and by sex (males > females, large effect, d = 0.94), with a significant species×sex interaction — the male-female gap is largest in Gentoo.
  2. Flipper length also differs significantly by species, and does so more completely than body mass: it distinguishes all three species pairwise, whereas body mass cannot separate Adelie from Chinstrap.
  3. Parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) and their non-parametric counterparts (Kruskal-Wallis) agree on every conclusion reached in this analysis, indicating the findings are robust to the mild normality/variance-homogeneity violations observed in a few subgroups.
